Below grade A section through the lot

Cut the lot open.

  1. Under the lawn

    Topsoil, clay, gravel. Everything the house depends on runs through the first three metres of it, and nobody sees any of it until something fails.

    Ask about what is under your lot ↗
  2. Water & gas

    Both arrive from the street. Water at about 2.4 m, gas at 1.3 m, each ending at a shut-off you should know how to find. Poly-B, lead and frozen services all fail here.

    Request plumbing ↗

    Every drain falls to a lateral that runs back to the lane main. Roots are the first cause of a blocked line, and a camera scope shows them before the basement does.

    Request a sewer scope ↗

    Weeping tile gathers water at the footing and sends it to the sump. One crack, a clogged tile or a dead pump, and the basement finds out first.

    Request a foundation check ↗

    Soil gas rises through the same ground and the warm house draws it in. A pit under the slab, a fan and a stack send it above the roof.

H-101 · 1 of 6 The 200 A panel

Open the panel.

  1. Where the house meets the grid

    Everything electrical in a home starts at one grey box in the basement. Keep scrolling and the camera goes inside it: the main, the buses, the breakers that feed a kitchen, a charger, a hot tub.

    Request electrical ↗
  2. Meter and mast

    The utility's side ends at the meter socket. From the weatherhead down, the mast, the socket and the service entrance cable are yours, and a 100 A socket is the first thing a 200 A upgrade replaces.

    Request a service assessment ↗
  3. The main breaker

    One two-pole breaker sets the limit for the whole house. Homes built before 1980 often have 60 or 100 A behind this cover. Heat pumps, chargers and suites are why 200 A is now the common upgrade.

    Request a panel upgrade ↗
  4. Bus bars and branch circuits

    Two copper buses carry 120 V each. Breakers clip on and split the house into circuits: 15 A for plugs and lights, 20 A for kitchens and baths, 30 to 60 A for the big appliances. Full panels get sub-panels, not tandems.

    Request wiring ↗
  5. The big loads

    A 40 A range, a 50 A EV charger, a 60 A hot tub with its own GFCI. Each needs a dedicated two-pole breaker, the right gauge of cable and a permit. Together they are what pushes a 100 A service over the line.

    Request an EV or hot tub circuit ↗
  6. What an inspector looks for

    Aluminum branch wiring from 1965 to 1978 needs rated connectors or copper pigtails at every device. Bedrooms need AFCI, wet areas need GFCI, and every breaker should match its wire. The panel tells the story of the house.

    Request an electrical inspection ↗
  7. Does your service fit?

    Switch loads on and off and watch the gauge in the panel.

    Request a load calculation ↗

H-105 · 2 of 6 Roof and attic

Under the shingles.

  1. The roof after a hailstorm

    Calgary sits in Canada's hail corridor. Bruised shingles lose their granules and fail years early. Photograph the roof, the vents and the siding straight after a storm; the claim is easiest while the damage is fresh.

    Request a roof inspection ↗
  2. Ice dam at the eave

    Heat leaking into the attic melts the snow from below. The water refreezes over the cold eave, builds a dam and backs up under the shingles. The fix is almost never on the roof: it is air sealing and insulation below it.

    Request an attic check ↗
  3. Attic rain

    In a cold snap, moist house air frosts on the underside of the deck and on every nail tip. A chinook thaws it all at once and it rains inside the attic. Bath fans, dryer vents and ceiling leaks are the usual sources.

    Request an attic rain assessment ↗
  4. Insulation and ventilation

    About 400 mm of insulation reaches R-50, but only if the air leaks below it are sealed first. Soffit vents in, ridge vent out, baffles to keep the channel open, and every fan duct ending at a roof cap instead of in the insulation.

    Request insulation and air sealing ↗
  5. Vermiculite

    Gold-brown, pebbly attic insulation from before 1990 may contain asbestos. Do not disturb it. Sample, test and plan abatement before anyone adds insulation or runs new wiring through it.

    Request asbestos testing ↗
  6. Solar on the right slope

    A newer roof with a clear south or west face is the moment to assess solar, before the next re-roof. Calgary issued thousands of solar permits in the last three years, and every array ends at the same panel in the basement.

    Request a solar assessment ↗
  7. Windows and siding

    Fogged glass, cold frames and rotten sills show up on the same wall the ice dam drips down. Window replacement, siding and decking are the exterior jobs that finish the envelope.

    Request a window quote ↗

H-109 · 3 of 6 The sewer lateral

Follow the line to the lane.

  1. One pipe, every angle

    Every drain in the house falls by gravity into one lateral that runs under the yard to the city main. It is yours to the property line. HAUSE Sewer is 40-plus sites about that one pipe, and this drawing walks its length.

    Request sewer help ↗
  2. Camera inspection

    A crawler goes in at the cleanout and records the whole run: material, joints, cracks, bellies, roots and where the line meets the main. Before buying a house, before digging, and before accepting any quote.

    Request a sewer scope ↗
  3. Roots

    Clay tile laterals from before 1980 have a joint every 60 to 90 cm. Roots find the water vapour at a joint, grow in, and catch everything that follows. The first sign is usually the lowest drain gurgling.

    Request root cutting ↗
  4. Jetting and cleaning

    A cable auger cuts a hole through a blockage. A hydro-jet nozzle at 4,000 psi pulls itself up the line and scours the pipe wall clean. Which one depends on the pipe material and what the camera found.

    Request drain cleaning ↗
  5. Trenchless repair

    Two ways to get a new pipe without digging up the yard. A resin-soaked liner is inverted into the old pipe and cured in place. Or a bursting head splits the old pipe while it pulls new HDPE behind it. Both need a camera before and after.

    Request trenchless options ↗
  6. Replace or connect new

    Collapsed pipe, a bad belly or a new infill build means open-cut: a trench to the main, new PVC on gravel, and a permitted connection at the property line. Written scopes make the quotes compare like for like.

    Request a replacement quote ↗
  7. Water service and weeping tile

    Two more pipes share the same trench. The water service freezes, bursts or is grey Poly-B. The weeping tile at the footing silts up, and the sump that drains it has to work on the night it rains.

    Request plumbing ↗

H-107 · 4 of 6 Radon

The one you cannot see.

  1. Where it comes from

    Uranium in the soil decays to radium, then to radon gas. The warm house draws it up through slab cracks, the wall-to-floor joint and the sump. No smell, no colour, and the second leading cause of lung cancer in Canada.

    Request radon testing ↗
  2. Test for three months

    An alpha-track detector on the lowest lived-in floor for 91 days in heating season. Health Canada's guideline is 200 Bq/m³. Prairie homes test high more often than most of the country, and the only way to know is the test.

    Order a long-term test ↗
  3. Sub-slab suction

    A core through the slab, a small pit in the gravel below it, and a 100 mm PVC riser. The fan creates a vacuum under the whole floor, so the gas goes to the pipe instead of the house. Cracks and the sump lid get sealed so the vacuum holds.

    Request a mitigation quote ↗
  4. The fan and the stack

    An inline fan draws 50 to 90 W, runs for years, and sits in the attic or outside so any leak is downstream of the living space. The exhaust ends above the eave, away from windows. A U-tube manometer on the riser shows it is pulling.

    Request a fan replacement ↗
  5. Retest, then relax

    A C-NRPP certified mitigator retests after the system runs. Most homes drop below 100 Bq/m³. Calgary, Edmonton, Airdrie, Cochrane and Okotoks each have a HAUSE radon site, and the national ones cover the rest.

    Request a post-mitigation test ↗

H-117 · 5 of 6 After the disaster

The first 72 hours.

  1. Hour zero: water

    A burst supply line puts 20 litres a minute on the floor. Shut off the main, kill power to the area, move what you can, and photograph everything before the first wet thing leaves the house. The insurer will ask.

    Request emergency water cleanup ↗
  2. Sewage

    When the lateral backs up, the lowest drain is the one that overflows. This is category 3 water. Everything porous it touched goes out, and the sewer line that caused it gets scoped before the floor goes back in.

    Request sewage cleanup ↗
  3. Fire and smoke

    The fire is out in minutes. The smoke keeps working for days: soot settles on every surface above the fire, acids etch glass and metal, and odour soaks into drywall and ducts. Sprinklers, where they exist, change the whole story.

    Request fire restoration ↗
  4. Mould and hazmat

    Wet drywall grows mould in 48 to 72 hours. Past a few square metres it means containment, negative air and HEPA scrubbers. Chemical, biohazard and industrial cleanups follow the same rules with different gear.

    Request mould remediation ↗
  5. Asbestos before demolition

    Textured ceilings, 9×9 floor tiles, pipe wrap and vermiculite from before 1990 are tested before anyone swings a hammer. Alberta requires it on most renovation and demolition work. A lab result costs less than a stop-work order.

    Request asbestos testing ↗
  6. Dry, clean, rebuild

    Air movers and dehumidifiers for three to five days, moisture readings on the framing, then the trades come back in order: drywall, paint, floors, trim, a deep clean. Six weeks from the hour-zero photo to the finished room.

    Request restoration ↗

H-111 · 6 of 6 Twenty-five years, five verbs

One furnace. Every verb.

  1. Install · year 0

    Crated, levelled, gas and venting connected, permit closed. First-time additions are their own trade: the right opening, the right circuit, the right drain, before anything is unboxed. Installing.ca owns the day-zero jobs.

    Request an installation ↗
  2. Maintain · years 1 to 12

    A filter every three months, a tune-up every autumn, ducts cleaned when the renovation dust settles. The cheapest years in a furnace's life are the ones somebody looked after. Maintaining.ca is the calendar.

    Request a maintenance visit ↗
  3. Upgrade · year 12

    The furnace still works, so make it work better: a smart thermostat, a cold-climate heat pump beside it, a bigger panel to feed it. Upgrading.ca is 200 named improvements to a home you are keeping.

    Request an upgrade ↗
  4. Replace · year 18

    The tank rusts at the base one winter morning. Out with the 189 L tank, in with a tankless unit on the wall, or a new tank sized for the family. Replacing.ca and a dozen water-heater and furnace sites cover the swap.

    Request a replacement ↗
  5. Remove · year 22

    Disconnect, cap the gas, drain, dolly it out the door, recycle the steel. Removal is a trade with its own hazards and its own site. The space it leaves is where the next idea starts.

    Request a removal ↗
  6. Demolish · year 25

    The basement becomes a legal suite. Asbestos test, permits, selective demolition, a bin in the driveway, and the whole cycle starts again with a new install. Demolishing.ca plans the strip-out so the rebuild has somewhere to go.

    Request demolition planning ↗

    Standard Who gets to work in your home

    We take the guesswork out.

    Anyone can call themselves a contractor. A HAUSE pro has to prove it. Before a pro sees a single request, we check what most homeowners never get the chance to: licence, insurance, coverage, permits and the word of the people they've already worked for.

    • Licensed for the trade

      Where the work needs a licence or certificate, from electricians and gas fitters to plumbers, we check it with the body that issued it. A photo of a card doesn't count.

    • Insured to $2 million

      Commercial general liability insurance of at least $2 million, current and on file. If something goes wrong in your home, it's covered.

    • Covered by workers' comp

      WCB, WorkSafeBC or WSIB coverage in good standing, so an injury on your property never becomes your problem.

    • Permits pulled, properly

      Work that needs a permit gets one, in the pro's name. It matters at inspection, with your insurer and when you sell.

    • Highly rated

      4.5 stars or better from verified homeowners. We read the bad reviews too, and how the pro answered them.

    • Respected in the trade

      Recent references we can call, and a business record that holds up. Pros earn their place on reputation, not ad spend.

    • Checked again every year

      Insurance lapses and licences expire. Every pro is re-verified every 12 months and after any complaint.
